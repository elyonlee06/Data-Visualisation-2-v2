Malaysia rushes to get old

Our grandparents lived in a young country. Our grandchildren will live in an old one. France took 41 years to make that journey. America took 65. Malaysia is doing it in 23.

By Elyon Gabriel Lee

31? Technically older than half the country.

Take 100 random Malaysians for example. This would be the same crowd in 1970, now, and 2040.

The typical Malaysian was sixteen in 1970, thirty-one today, and will be thirty-seven by 2040 — but most of that change took fifty years, while the rest will take just sixteen. The country is ageing faster than it ever has.

The shape of the country is changing.

Watch the pyramid become a column. Where are the babies?

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM).

In 1980, Malaysia's population pyramid was a textbook example: a wide base of children, narrowing to a few elderly at the top. The country was making babies.

By 2024, the pyramid has flattened into a column. The bottom row, children under five, is smaller than the row of twenty-somethings above it. That has never happened before in Malaysia's recorded demographic history. Same country but different shape.

Births slow, deaths climb.

Every year, more Malaysians are born than die — but the gap is shrinking.

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM).

Each year, more Malaysians are born than die — but the gap is closing. In 2000, the country added roughly 437,000 net new Malaysians. In 2023, that figure dropped to 259,000. Deaths are climbing as the population ages. Births are slipping for a different reason.

Both lines on the chart above are counts — how many Malaysians, total, in a given year. The next chart shows the rate: how many children a typical Malaysian woman will have across her lifetime.

From six children to fewer than two.

No more kids?

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM).

In 1961, a Malaysian woman would have, on average, 6 children across her lifetime. Today, she has fewer than 2. That is below the "replacement rate" of 2.1 — the number needed to keep a population stable over generations without immigration.

Fewer children, smaller pyramid, ageing country.

Rich countries had 100 years. Malaysia has 23.

How does our pace of ageing compare to the rest of the world?

Loading…

Sources: UN World Population Prospects 2024 (via Our World in Data); Natural Earth boundaries.

Demographers measure the "speed" of an ageing transition as the number of years a country takes to go from 7% elderly to 14% elderly. France crossed 7% in 1864 and 14% in 1979 — 115 years. The United States took 65. Sweden took 22.

Malaysia crossed 7% in 2022. The UN projects we will reach 14% by 2045. That is 23 years. Roughly the same as China and Sweden, faster than Japan, almost three times as fast as the US. We are in the world's fast-ageing tier — and so is most of our neighbourhood.

Most countries take more than 30 years to make the same transition. Malaysia gets 23.

Pause. The next chart is good news. Malaysia's ageing isn't really a story of decline — it's a story of survival.

Malaysia improved alongside the world.

From 57 years in 1960 to 77 in 2023 — a gain of nearly 20 years in one lifetime.

Loading…

Source: Our World in Data (Human Mortality Database + UN WPP 2024).

We're not alone. Almost every country in the world is living longer than in 1960, including Malaysia. A baby born here in 1960 was expected to live 57 years. A baby born today expects 77. Twenty extra years, in one human lifetime.

That's good news. Better health, better medicine, fewer babies dying. But it's also why the country is ageing — and the consequences will land unevenly across our states. That's where this story goes next.

Not every Malaysia is ageing equally.

Zoom in. Some states are already old. Others are still young.

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM). Map: geoBoundaries (CC-BY 4.0).

In Perak, almost one in ten residents is already aged 65 or older. Kedah and Sarawak are close behind. These are states where children grew up, moved away to KL or Singapore for work, and left their parents in place.

At the other extreme: Putrajaya, the planned administrative capital, is the youngest place in Malaysia at 1.9% — full of civil servants and young families who moved in over the last 25 years. Sabah stays young for a different reason: a large migrant workforce and a higher birth rate. The map captures both stories.

Every state aged — at different speeds.

Which state aged the fastest?

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM). Putrajaya excluded — only founded in 2001.

Every Malaysian state has aged since 2000. But the speed varies. Selangor's elderly share grew the most — nearly tripling from 2.8% to 8.0%, a 5.3-point jump in twenty-four years.

Eleven states gained more than three percentage points. Malacca, oddly, gained the least — just 1.5 points. The transition is everywhere, just not at the same pace.

The demographic dividend is ending.

For 30 years, Malaysia has had more workers than dependents than ever before. That window is closing.

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (historical, 1970–2025) + UN World Population Prospects 2024 (projection, 2026–2040).

Demographers call it the demographic dividend: the few decades in a country's transition when there are lots of working adults and relatively few children or elderly to support. Malaysia entered the dividend in the 1990s and has been enjoying it since.

By 2040, the workers' share will plateau at about 71%. The children's share will continue shrinking. The elderly share will more than quadruple from where it was in 1970. By 2040, roughly one in eight Malaysians will be aged 65+. By 2060, demographers project it will be closer to one in four.

Two different problems, on the same map.

Ageing isn't one challenge — it is two. Scale and intensity. They land in different places.

Loading…

Source: Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), 2024. Map: geoBoundaries (CC-BY 4.0).

Selangor has the most elderly people in absolute terms — almost 600,000 — but it also has the country's largest workforce to support them. Perak has fewer elderly in absolute numbers, but its workforce is the most stretched: roughly 14 elderly for every 100 working-age adults.

Solving for absolute scale points policy toward Selangor and Johor: eldercare housing, retirement income, hospital capacity. Solving for relative strain points toward Perak and Kedah: workforce shortages, productivity, replacement migration.

Same country, same challenge. Two very different places where it lands.

Malaysia's ageing isn't one problem. It's two.
